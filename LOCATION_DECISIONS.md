# Location Decisions

- Location will follow the existing dimension architecture with a `Location` model, `LocationState`, and `LocationDimension`. A `LocationCatalog` will construct the product-specific locations and their static configuration.
- Locations are product-specific. Each product has its own location progression, presentation assets, operating calendar, events, and relocation requirements.
- Location-specific special events are deferred until after the initial game
  release. The operating-season and location architecture should leave room
  for fixed annual events and conditional events such as playoffs, but event
  scheduling, persistence, gameplay effects, and presentation are outside the
  current location feature scope.
- Relocation preserves the player's business progression, but resets the calendar and all ingredient quantities. Ingredient upgrades, recipe and lifespan multipliers, replacement-ingredient configuration, and the equipment that enabled them persist.
- Relocation takes effect at the end of the current day for every phase transition. Players will not be required to wait until the end of an operating season.
- The location persistence schema will be finalized after the runtime model is implemented. Static catalog data will be rebuilt on restore; only dynamic state that cannot be reconstructed will be saved.
- When a seasonal location reaches the end of its annual operating window, play continues into the next season at the same location. The calendar wraps to the following year's opening date and all ingredient quantities are cleared, while ingredient upgrades and configuration persist.
- Relocation is recorded as a business event, and each day summary stores its location ID so historical reporting can filter, group, or traverse results across locations without inferring boundaries from events.
- Business reputation will continue using the Location Tier 1 market-size calculation at every location. Reputation is a continuous, smoothed measure rather than a tiered purchasable upgrade, and its current `0.15` market-size weight already provides a modest range of approximately -9.9% to +11.0%. Expanding that range in later locations would make reputation disproportionately influential and would complicate an otherwise understandable score. Phase 2 market-size growth will instead be allocated entirely among the tiered upgrade dimensions available in that phase, whose Phase 2 weights must total `1.0` without including reputation.
- Tiered market-size upgrades use a location-adjusted target multiplier instead of a uniform effect score. A uniform effect score assumes every star represents the same portion of the entire progression, while the target multiplier reflects how much growth remains in each location tier and divides that growth evenly among the stars available in that tier. This makes later-location stars more valuable when they must cover a larger portion of the market-size progression. The resulting target multiplier remains unweighted with respect to any individual dimension; advertising, transportation, and other dimension weights are applied separately afterward. In other words, location determines the value represented by each star, while the dimension weight determines that dimension's share of the resulting market-size growth.
- Upgrade pricing uses a representative market-size multiplier of `1.0` for Location Tier 1 or location-agnostic upgrades and `1.5` for Location Tier 2 upgrades. This estimate supplies the market context when pricing demand benefits; it is not the player's actual market size. When an upgrade itself affects market size, that portion of its price uses the upgrade's actual starting and ending target multipliers instead. Labor and other location-agnostic upgrades therefore continue to use the Location Tier 1 pricing assumption.
- Advertisement weekly prices are currently high relative to comparable one-time prices because recurring pricing charges a substantial portion of the estimated daily benefit every business week. Validate during gameplay testing whether these ongoing costs remain worthwhile at their intended phase before changing the formula.
- Tier 5 advertising can cost less per week than some Tier 4 options. This is acceptable and can be realistic because advertising channels are alternatives with different costs and benefits, not progressively better versions of the same asset. Advertising prices do not need to increase monotonically by tier.
- Primary-equipment capacity follows the location progression `90% → 130% → 170% → 210% → 270% → 330%`. The three Location Tier 1 purchases reach 210%, and the two Location Tier 2 purchases add 60 percentage points each to reach 330%. Labor and storage will be updated separately rather than as part of this change.
- Production-capacity balancing is centralized through `CapacitySchedule`. A `CapacityUpgrade` connects a stable `CapacityUpgradeID` to a scheduled capacity ratio, and `CapacitySchedule.capacity(for:product:)` converts that ratio into rounded whole units using the product's ideal unit sales. Each catalog owns the schedule for its dimension: equipment uses separate primary and secondary schedules, while labor uses one schedule containing its `120%` player baseline, `90%` specialist contribution, and `40%` shared-worker contribution. The caller still determines whether the returned capacity replaces an existing total or adds to it, and location availability remains outside the capacity schedule.
- Tiered upgrades remain visible before their required location level but cannot be purchased. Purchase validation checks the active location tier before finances so players see `Available at Level 2` immediately rather than saving for an upgrade only to discover the location restriction later. Location-agnostic upgrades continue directly to financial validation.
- Purchase and relocation follow the same high-level transaction sequence—capture a snapshot, apply changes, save, and revert on failure—but they affect different state and require different snapshot types. We considered a parent `Workflow` class with `PurchaseWorkflow` and `RelocationWorkflow` subclasses using overridden template methods. Instead, these workflows will use composition: each workflow retains its own domain-specific public API and delegates the shared atomic save-and-rollback sequence to a contained `GameStateTransaction`, supplying its specialized capture, apply, and revert behavior. This avoids artificial reference semantics, Swift's lack of true abstract class methods, single-inheritance constraints, and exposing irrelevant parent behavior. A protocol can be added later if the workflows need to be treated polymorphically as one public workflow family.
- TODO: Perform a deeper architectural review of composition versus inheritance, including protocol default implementations, closure-based dependency injection, substitutability, testing implications, and when a shared workflow abstraction is genuinely useful.
- Dimension availability and individual item availability are separate backend concepts. `dimensionAvailability` evaluates a broad restriction that affects the whole dimension, such as the calendar or `UpgradeTracker` restriction on an entire purchase category. `itemAvailability` evaluates one particular item, such as an upgrade's location and financial requirements or a destination's relocation requirements. Both checks remain explicit requirements of the shared `Workflow` protocol. Whether dimension unavailability blocks entry to a view or instead opens it in a read-only state remains a frontend decision that can change without altering these backend rules.

## Operating-Period Lifecycle Instead of a Large Relocation Snapshot

The first relocation design treated every immediate operational consequence as part of one large transaction. Before relocating, `RelocationWorkflow` would snapshot the active location, actual and displayed balances, ingredient quantities and age data, calendar fields, weather, pending business events, and any other state changed by the new location. It would then apply the relocation, clear inventory, jump the calendar, generate weather, save the resulting game, and restore every captured field if saving failed. Location-dependent values such as business hours, available upgrades, backgrounds, customers, demand, and market size also appeared at first to require mutation or rollback.

That approach would work, but every new location behavior would enlarge the snapshot and require matching capture and restoration logic. It created multiple copies of state that can already be derived from the active location and made relocation responsible for coordinating inventory, weather, calendar, presentation, and future seasonal events. A missed field could leave the game partially relocated after a save failure.

The revised design separates the durable relocation transaction from repeat-safe operating-period initialization. Location-dependent behavior is calculated from the active location instead of being copied into mutable state. Relocation changes only its true transaction state: the active location, financial reservation, pending business event, and calendar operating-period transition. It sets `operatingPeriodDay` to `0` and saves that state. An operating-period day of zero is a persisted transition marker meaning that the next operating period has not yet been prepared.

Whenever the game encounters `operatingPeriodDay == 0`, the shared operating-period-start flow begins the next period. `GameCalendar.beginOperatingPeriod(product:)` creates the new `OperatingPeriod`, resolves the product-specific operating dates and appropriate year, adjusts weekend boundaries, sets the global simulation-day boundaries, moves `currentDate` to the period start, and changes `operatingPeriodDay` to `1`. The higher-level start flow then expires ingredient quantities, generates the opening weather forecast, initializes future period-specific events, and saves the prepared state.

This same lifecycle handles three formerly separate cases: starting a new game, relocating to the next location, and rolling a location into its next annual operating window. Finite operating periods store their business-day length. After the final playable day, normal day advancement moves `operatingPeriodDay` beyond that length and converts it to `0`; the next-day flow then starts the next annual operating period. The first home operating period has no length and therefore continues indefinitely until relocation.

The day-zero state also makes the transition recoverable. If the app closes after relocation or operating-period rollover is saved but before initialization completes, restoration sees `operatingPeriodDay == 0` and safely attempts initialization again. Operations placed in the operating-period-start flow must therefore be safe to repeat. Financial charges, business-event creation, counters, and other exactly-once mutations remain in the relocation transaction and must not be repeated by operating-period initialization.

This reduces the relocation rollback snapshot substantially and establishes one extensible lifecycle for future calendar jumps, inventory expiration, forecasts, and special events. The core rule is: persist the transition first, then derive and prepare the new operating period through the recoverable day-zero path.
